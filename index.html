Packing Unit Squares

Pack n unit squares into the smallest possible square container. An old open problem, built up from scratch.

The challenge. For each n from 1 to 1,000, output a centre (x, y) and a rotation angle (radians) for n squares of side 1. Rules: interiors must not overlap (touching is fine), and the score is L, the side of the tightest axis-aligned square container around them. Smaller L is better. Only a handful of n have proven optimal answers; for the rest, any tighter L is a new record.

1The area bound

n unit squares cover area n. A container of side L has area L². So no packing can beat

L ≥ √n

√n is usually not a whole number, so it is only a lower bound: the best L can never be below it, but it is rarely reachable. The dashed square below has side √n. The solid container is the simple grid, with ⌈√n⌉ squares per row, where ⌈√n⌉ means √n rounded up to a whole number.

2How much room is there to improve?

The grid wastes ⌈√n⌉² − n cells. For n = k² it is perfect (and optimal). For n = k² + 1 the grid jumps to side k + 1, yet the area bound √n is only a little above k. That gap, shown below, is what clever (often tilted) packings try to eat.

Each bar is the gap ⌈√n⌉ − √n (grid side minus area bound) for n = 1…50; the blue bar is the n chosen in section 1.

Two known facts: n = k² − 1 and n = k² − 2 can’t beat the grid (L = k, proven by Nagamochi), so the interesting n are just above a perfect square, such as 5, 10, 17, 26.

3Why tilting helps: n = 5

Grid needs L = 3 for 5 squares. Instead, put four squares in the corners and rotate the fifth by 45° in the middle. Along the container’s diagonal you pass a corner square (√2), the diamond (side 1) and the other corner square (√2):

L·√2 = √2 + 1 + √2  ⟹  L = 2 + 1/√2 ≈ 2.7071

Drag the angle. At the wrong angle the middle square pokes into the corner squares (red).

4Playground: a tiny annealing solver

This is the same recipe the challenge suggests: treat overlap as an energy, jiggle squares randomly (simulated annealing), and whenever the layout is valid, shrink the container a little and repair. You can also drag squares by hand and rotate the selected one.

It is a toy: it will often stall above the best known L, and the gap shows why this is hard. Serious entries add smarter moves, exact contact constraints and long runs.

For large n the story changes: good packings are mostly a perfect grid with a few rows or strips tilted slightly, and Erdős and Graham showed the wasted area grows more slowly than the container area. That is the regime the n ≈ 1,000 puzzles live in.
